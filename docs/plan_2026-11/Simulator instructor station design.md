# Show the truth, the telemetry and the gap

An instructor console for C2O should be built around three things that sit on screen at all times and one thing that changes. The three fixed elements are a grey **overview strip** of subsystem health, a **single shared timeline** carrying scenario phase, injected faults and trainee actions, and a **truth-versus-telemetry layer** that shows where what the trainees see differs from what the simulator knows. The changing element is a **focus pane**. It follows events (a fault injected, a phase change, an unacknowledged alarm) and says why it moved, and the instructor can pin it or go back in one click. The reason to build this rather than copy existing space practice is that none of the documented space simulators provide it. ESA's SIMSAT/SIMULUS consoles are engineering tools: an object tree, variable editing, plots, logs, scheduled scripts and snapshots. NASA's SimSup practice lives in people and scripts, not in a described console. NOS3 injects faults by commanding hardware simulators directly through a back channel. Aviation, nuclear, maritime and medical simulators converge on a common instructor feature set: initial-condition libraries, freeze and backtrack, timed or conditional malfunctions with severity and ramp, sensor overrides kept separate from real faults, event logs and debrief. The 1991 nuclear certification is the most complete written specification of that set. The debriefing evidence points to a structured, timeline-based debrief rather than video. Simulation plus debriefing beats no intervention across 177 studies, but video adds no measurable benefit. The evidence on automatic view switching argues for suggest-and-pin focus changes rather than full automation. Most of the specific display designs below have not been tested on instructor stations. They are design hypotheses drawn from adjacent evidence, and the report marks them as such.

## Space agencies run simulations with debug consoles and devious people

The European reference is SIMULUS, the basis for all of ESOC's ground and space segment operational simulators. It amounts to **about 2 million lines of code across 10 separate developments**, built over 15 years ([Steele & Reggestad, SESP 2017](https://indico.esa.int/event/180/contributions/1355/attachments/1271/1496/1500_Steele_-_Paper.pdf)). Its operator layer is the SIMSAT MMI. Published descriptions show an Explorer with a hierarchical object view and each model's parameters, services and variables, multiple filtered log viewers, real-time plots, scheduled commands and Java scripts, a Time Keeper, and a Storer that saves and reloads simulation states under the name **"breakpointing"** ([Whitty, Terma, SIMSAT 4.3](https://indico.esa.int/event/109/contributions/169/attachments/302/340/2020093_whitty.pdf)). In SIMSAT a "breakpoint" is a state snapshot, not a debugger halt. That terminology trap matters if C2O borrows the word. Terma's current description of SIMSAT calls it a kernel providing "the scheduling, MMI, control interfaces" for training flight control teams on LEOP and contingencies, but says nothing about how failures are injected or what the instructor sees ([Terma](https://www.terma.com/markets/space/ground-segment/satellite-simulators/)).

ESOC's own improvement study shows where it thought the operator console fell short. The 2016–18 SIMULUS NG study prototyped breakpointing **"without pausing the simulator"**, subtree breakpoints, breakpoint transfer across constellation members, **forecast mode and backward run**, per-site control in the MMI, "status definition and dumping", and visualisation of rotations and vectors ([Steele, SESP 2017 presentation](https://indico.esa.int/event/180/contributions/1355/attachments/1270/1495/1500_Steele_Presentation.pdf)). Taken together, these are requests for better time control and better situational awareness of the model. Whether they shipped in SIMULUS 10 or 11 is not known.

The pedagogical function is carried by a person. For SMART-1's roughly ten pre-launch simulations, the simulation officer was "chosen… for being devious and cunning" and "intentionally injects simulated failures". Simple failures were handled with contingency procedures, and harder ones escalated to a joint decision by the FOD and Mission Director ([ESA SMART-1](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/SMART-1/SMART-1_simulation_campaign)). Telespazio's commercial training centre offers the same role as a service: simulation officers who write nominal and contingency scenarios and run tailored training plans ([Telespazio](https://telespazio.de/en/news-and-stories-detail/-/detail/dlr-school-lab-tu-darmstadt)). Simulator time is also scarce. At DLR GSOC, few simulators exist, **"validation of flight operations procedures (FOP) on the DSS takes precedence over training"**, and the centre built a board game to practise anomaly handling partly for that reason ([Jaus & Lautenschuetz, SpaceOps 2025](https://publications.spaceops.org/2025/download_by_id.php?id=0234)). A web simulator that many students can share removes that bottleneck, but only if one instructor can run it without an engineering team behind them.

NASA's practice is richer in scenario craft than in tooling. The Sim Sup assembles malfunctions into the "optimum combination" that pushes a team to its edge without making the case unwinnable. Simulated MCC data looks exactly like flight data. One shuttle ascent sim packed **47 malfunctions into 10 minutes** to teach triage. Some injects happened off the computer, until a note simulating a controller's heart attack led management to ban that kind ([Wayne Hale](https://blogs.nasa.gov/waynehalesblog/2010/02/16/post_1266353065166)). For the LADEE and NuSTAR robotic missions, Ames organised faults by **control-loop element**: control input (an accidental safe-mode uplink, an operator "declared absent"), controller (a sign error in the wheel control law), actuator (a wheel short), process input (off-nominal insertion, degraded DSN), disturbance (SEUs), process (solar cell damage), observer (stuck or biased sensors) and tracking data. The SimSup also had to decide what to do with unplanned simulator glitches ("freebies"): ignore them, restart, or work them as real ([Owens & Crocker, NTRS 20150021505](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150021505/downloads/20150021505.pdf)). The open-source NOS3 has no instructor station at all. Faults are set by sending commands to hardware simulators over a `SIM_CMD_BUS_BRIDGE` that "bypasses flight software", and the 42 3D camera window serves as the truth display ([NOS3 docs](https://nos3.readthedocs.io/en/v1_07_03/Scenario_Demo.html)).

Two conclusions follow. First, no published space source describes a purpose-built instructor console with curated views. The authoritative ESOC paper on running SIM campaigns (AIAA 2006-5914) could not be retrieved, so this gap may partly reflect access rather than practice. Second, every system injects through a direct "god channel" to model state while the trainees' ground system sees only the consequences. That makes the truth-versus-telemetry distinction the core of the console, not an extra.

## Other industries converge on one instructor feature set

High-fidelity simulators outside space show a stable common core, and the nuclear specification states it most precisely. The 1991 Shearon Harris operator-training simulator certification includes the following ([NRC ML14188B169](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML1418/ML14188B169.pdf)):

- freeze/run and backtrack
- **50 initial-condition slots in tiers**: permanent and password-protected, instructor-maintained, maintenance, a default snapshot, and a dedicated "return to interrupted scenario" slot
- malfunctions inserted by number or menu, at any number and simultaneously or on a schedule, with **severity and ramp time**, triggerable on plant conditions, and with pending malfunctions reviewable at any time
- **device and transmitter overrides kept separate from malfunctions**
- fast time for slow processes only, and slow time "so trends are easier to observe"
- **alerts when the simulation leaves its validated envelope**
- up to 20 automated lesson drills

Maritime and medical vendors add role and progression features. Kongsberg's K-Sim lets instructors define "exactly which information is accessible and visible" at each student station. It supports malfunctions that are instantaneous or pre-programmed, triggers with "e-coach" messages to students, full recording, and rewind-and-resume ([Kongsberg](https://www.kongsberg.com/maritime/products2/solutions/instructor-system)). Laerdal's LLEAP separates a **manual mode** run on the fly from an **automatic mode** in which logged and detected trainee events advance the scenario. It adds "tap to log" event keys feeding an integrated debrief ([Laerdal LLEAP](https://cdn.laerdal.com/downloads/f3133/14-14353_LLEAP_SS_final.pdf)). CM Labs' crane-simulator IOS adds instructor bookmarks and a live composite score ([CM Labs](https://www.cm-labs.com/news/cm-labs-releases-all-in-one-solution-for-monitoring-assessing-and-engaging-trainees/)).

The cautionary evidence is old but specific. A 1983 US Navy review of the F-14A weapon system trainer console found the following ([DTIC ADA136965](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA136965.pdf)):

- **162 display pages**, with editing an initial condition sometimes requiring "literally all display pages"
- a systems index that "does not logically parallel the aircrew's picture of the consoles"
- monitoring data competing with control data for screen space
- a 10-page malfunction list in which simultaneous malfunctions could not be started by hand
- a replay that was "seldom used" because it consumed training time
- an event monitor that showed only the last 20 actions and was "rarely used"
- no console position at all for the battle-problem controller

Instructors fell back on repeater displays of the trainee's own view. Regulation does not fix this. 14 CFR Part 60 requires that instructor-station functions be described and checked, but the retrieved text sets no usability criteria ([14 CFR Part 60](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2012-title14-vol2/pdf/CFR-2012-title14-vol2-part60.pdf)). The evidence-backed fix is structural. Branaghan and colleagues rebuilt an IOS menu from card sorts of domain experts' knowledge, used the proximity-compatibility principle, and validated the result with timed tasks ([Applied Ergonomics 2011](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/using-knowledge-structures-to-redesign-an-instructor-operator-sta/)). A Naval Postgraduate School study of touch input concluded that input devices "should be matched with the tasks performed" ([DTIC ADA239524](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/html/tr/ADA239524/index.html)).

| Common instructor function | Strongest documented precedent | What it means for C2O |
|---|---|---|
| Tiered IC/snapshot library, "return to interrupted" slot | Nuclear 1991 | Locked scenario starts, instructor saves, an automatic pre-inject snapshot |
| Freeze, backtrack, fast/slow time | Nuclear 1991; SIMSAT; TS21 1–25x | Time controls on the timeline; label what acceleration skips |
| Malfunction vs override, severity, ramp, trigger | Nuclear 1991; TS21 CMG | Two inject classes: physical fault vs telemetry/sensor lie |
| Pending-inject review | Nuclear 1991 | Upcoming injects drawn ahead of "now" on the timeline |
| Per-station visibility control | Kongsberg | Instructor-only views and loops never routed to student sessions |
| Event-driven scenario progression | Laerdal automatic mode | Phase state machine advanced by detected trainee actions |
| Logging, bookmarks, debrief | Laerdal, CM Labs, Kongsberg | One event log feeding a PEARLS-structured debrief |
| Model-validity alert | Nuclear §1.3.7.9 | Warn when the sim leaves its validated envelope, separate from trainee alerts |

## Truth versus indication is the console's central view

The best space evidence for what an instructor must see comes from model design, not UI design. The ISS double-gimbal CMG malfunction models are driven by activation flags and parameters, with activation at set sim times. Some faults are **telemetry-only**: a Hall-sensor failure shows a user-set wheel speed on the displays while the actual wheel speed is unchanged ([Inampudi & Gordeuk, NTRS 20150021963](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150021963/downloads/20150021963.pdf)). In the Ames campaigns, sensor injects made indications differ from the true state, and trainees were expected to find the truth from other indications ([Owens & Crocker](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150021505/downloads/20150021505.pdf)). The nuclear separation of malfunctions from transmitter overrides is the same idea in another industry ([NRC](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML1418/ML14188B169.pdf)). An instructor who cannot see both values at once cannot tell whether a team is stuck because the failure is physically severe or because they believe a lying sensor. Neither NOS3 nor SIMSAT shows the two side by side. Truth appears only in engineering tools (the 42 viewer, the object explorer) ([NOS3](https://nos3.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Scenario_Demo.html)).

The recommended pattern is a **paired-value cell**: truth, last telemetered value, and age of that value, with divergence highlighted only above a threshold. Precedents exist for showing staleness but not divergence. COSMOS colours stale or missing values pink, and Open MCT ships a staleness plugin plus DerivedTelemetry and CorrelationTelemetry, which could compute a divergence channel ([NOS3](https://nos3.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Scenario_Demo.html); [Open MCT plugins.js](https://github.com/nasa/openmct/blob/master/src/plugins/plugins.js)). For geometry, the same idea becomes a truth body with a translucent "estimate" ghost in the attitude view, plus a scalar attitude-error sparkline, because 3D views are poor for reading magnitudes. For C2O the divergence has three sources that the cell should tell apart. One is injected sensor or telemetry faults. Another is the information delay that is legitimate in operations: no contact, delayed TM, or a packet store not yet dumped. The third is onboard-estimate error, such as attitude determination or OBC clock drift. Only the first is the instructor's doing, and only the first should be drawn in the inject colour. This three-way split is a design proposal. No study has evaluated divergence highlighting on an instructor station.

Fault injection and propagation need a different view: a causal graph. Ecological Interface Design is built on Rasmussen's abstraction hierarchy, which runs from physical form through generalised function to functional purpose and links levels by "why/how" relations. EID makes broken constraints perceptually visible ([EID overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_interface_design)). A 30-year review found it "remarkably successful in significantly improving performance" in law-driven domains like process control ([Bennett & Flach 2019](https://corescholar.libraries.wright.edu/psychology/665)). Spacecraft subsystems are law-driven, so a propagation view shaped like that hierarchy fits. It would show the injected fault at the component, the functions it degrades (energy balance, pointing, downlink), and the mission objective at risk. A **fault swimlane** sits alongside it with the timestamps that matter for teaching: inserted, first symptom in telemetry, first alarm to trainees, acknowledged, diagnosed, recovered. The gaps between those marks are the performance measures. The Ames control-loop taxonomy should organise the inject catalogue itself (input, controller, actuator, process, disturbance, observer, data products). That replaces the F-14's ten-page list with a structure that also teaches the logic the SimSup explained in debriefs ([Owens & Crocker](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150021505/downloads/20150021505.pdf)).

The truth views themselves follow the high-performance HMI hierarchy. Level 1 is an overview of the whole span of responsibility built from pattern-recognition objects. Level 2 covers units, Level 3 component control graphics, and Level 4 detail. Normal state is grey and colour is reserved for abnormality ([Allen 2017](https://www.automation.com/article/modern-dcs-graphics-level-1-overview-displays); [ABB 2016](https://new.abb.com/power-generation/in-control/01-2016/the-key-to-operator-effectiveness)). ABB reports that high-performance HMIs let operators detect abnormal situations before alarms **48% vs 10%** of the time and succeed **96% vs 70%**. Those are vendor figures with the underlying study unnamed, so treat them as directional. The per-subsystem pictures already proposed for the operator consoles carry over unchanged, as long as they read from truth: a power flow mimic, a thermal node network, an RF link waterfall, an OBSW mode diagram, and packet-store fill bars. For the mode diagram the instructor needs **the last transitions and what triggered them**, not just the current mode. Sarter and Woods identify indirect mode transitions as a main cause of automation surprises, and FDIR-driven safe-mode entries are exactly that ([Hebert on Sarter & Woods](https://ferd.ca/notes/paper-how-in-the-world-did-we-ever-get-into-that-mode.html)).

| Information need | Primary view | Supporting detail |
|---|---|---|
| Whole-sim health at a glance | Grey L1 strip, one tile per subsystem, sparkline + worst state | Orbit/eclipse/contact ribbon on the shared time axis |
| Truth vs what trainees see | Paired cells (truth / TM / age), divergence flag by cause | Attitude truth body + estimate ghost |
| Fault and propagation | Abstraction-hierarchy causal graph | Fault swimlane: inserted → symptom → alarm → ack → recovered |
| Scenario timeline and phase | Phase state machine above a shared timeline | Pending injects ahead of "now", conditional triggers shown as gates |
| Trainee activity | Swimlanes: TCs sent, alarms acked, procedure steps, voice-loop activity | Per-team load indicator (open alarms, TC rate, procedure progress) |
| Model validity | Separate envelope-alert channel | Never mixed with trainee-performance alerts |
| Debrief | Seekable replay of the same timeline with markers | PEARLS prompt sheet, plus/delta capture |

## One timeline carries scenario, faults and trainee actions, then becomes the debrief

The design choice with the most leverage is putting everything on one time axis. The F-14 console split events across a 20-entry actuation monitor, a malfunction list and an unused replay, and the instructors stopped using all three ([DTIC ADA136965](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA136965.pdf)). A single timeline with swimlanes avoids that split. The lanes would be scenario phase, scheduled and fired injects, alarms raised and acknowledged, telecommands sent and verified, procedure steps, and voice-loop activity. It gives the instructor live situational awareness and is already the debrief record. LLEAP's automatic mode shows the progression logic. Scenario phases advance on detected trainee events, which in C2O terms is a phase state machine whose transitions are telecommands, acknowledgements or spacecraft mode changes ([Laerdal](https://cdn.laerdal.com/downloads/f3133/14-14353_LLEAP_SS_final.pdf)). Conditional injects become gates drawn on the timeline (for example, "inject battery-cell short when the team commands payload ON"), and pending injects are visible ahead of "now", as the nuclear spec requires ([NRC](https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML1418/ML14188B169.pdf)). Time acceleration should be shown on the axis with its side effects labelled. TS21's 1–25x CMG speed-up skipped telemetry bit tests while active, and that is exactly the kind of hidden fidelity loss that should appear on screen ([NTRS 20150021963](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150021963/downloads/20150021963.pdf)).

The debriefing evidence decides what to spend on. A meta-analysis of **177 studies and 11,511 learners** found simulation plus debriefing beat no intervention on every outcome (effect sizes 0.28–2.16). **Video-assisted debriefing showed no significant advantage** over debriefing without video (ES 0.10, 4 studies) ([Cheng et al. 2014](https://read.qxmd.com/read/24909527/debriefing-for-technology-enhanced-simulation-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis)). PEARLS structures the debrief in four phases: Reactions, Description (building a shared picture of key events), Analysis and Summary. It matches each performance gap to self-assessment, focused facilitation, or directive teaching. In the EXPRESS trial, **novice instructors with a script** produced greater learner knowledge gains ([Eppich & Cheng 2015](https://case.edu/nursing/sites/default/files/2018-05/Article-Eppich-PEARLS.pdf)). The authors call PEARLS's own benefits anecdotal, so the evidence supports scripting and structure in general more than this framework specifically. For C2O, the Description phase is the timeline replayed and the Analysis phase is the fault swimlane's latency gaps. A prompt sheet keyed to those gaps suits the many C2O sessions that will be led by teaching assistants or by students in practice mode rather than by an experienced SimSup. Replay must be seekable and semantic (telemetry, telecommands, events), not a real-time re-run. A replay that costs training time goes unused. Video and screen capture can wait. The evidence on debrief effect sizes and moderators from Keiser and Arthur's 2021 AAR meta-analysis and a 2024 technology-in-AAR review could not be retrieved, and newer nursing meta-analyses on video were not checked.

Trainee-activity views should measure process, not just outcome. Useful per-team signals are open unacknowledged alarms, telecommand rate, procedure progress against the expected step, and time since the last inject response. Valve's Left 4 Dead "AI Director" combines comparable signals into a per-player "stress level" and reshapes encounters around it ([Serviss 2013](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-discomfort-zone-the-hidden-potential-of-valve-s-ai-director)). A "director" panel would help the instructor decide when to hold or release the next fault, and the same signals could drive automatic injects in solo practice mode. That is an analogy, not validated evidence. **Missed-cue alerts** fit the same model, for example when a scripted condition is not met within a set time after an inject. They are the equivalent of Kongsberg's triggers and e-coach messages ([Kongsberg](https://www.kongsberg.com/maritime/products2/solutions/instructor-system)), but they must stay visually distinct from model-validity alerts.

## Switch the focus pane, never the overview

The evidence on automatic switching cuts both ways. In a NASA study, system-driven automation switching produced **20.3% lower subjective workload** than user-initiated switching. Operators left to choose used automation only about **7%** of the time, and frequent manual switching tracked with worse performance (R² = .693) ([Bailey et al., NTRS 20040084079](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20040084079/downloads/20040084079.pdf)). Purely manual navigation, the F-14 problem, is therefore costly. Automatic changes bring their own failure: Sarter and Woods found that automation surprises cluster in non-normal, time-critical situations, that indirect transitions cause them, and that interfaces "show state but not context" ([Hebert on Sarter & Woods](https://ferd.ca/notes/paper-how-in-the-world-did-we-ever-get-into-that-mode.html)). In adaptive interfaces, accuracy drove use (86.4% vs 70.6% utilisation at 70% vs 50% accuracy). Unpredictability did not hurt speed but did hurt perceived control, and many users wanted adapted items to stay near their original locations ([Gajos et al., CHI 2008](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/kgajos-chi08-predictability.pdf)). ISA-101 practice warns that drill-down creates a "keyhole" effect, and counters it with a persistent Level 1 overview ([Allen 2017](https://www.automation.com/article/modern-dcs-graphics-level-1-overview-displays)).

These findings lead to a **semi-automatic focus rule**:

- The overview strip and timeline never move.
- Only one focus pane adapts, and it switches only on high-confidence events: an inject fired, a phase transition, a trainee opening a procedure, an alarm left unacknowledged beyond a threshold.
- Every switch is announced with its cause, for example "Focus → Power because EPS-07 fired at T+12:04".
- Switches are rate-limited so a cascade of symptoms does not churn the pane.
- The instructor can pin the pane, and a one-click "back" restores the previous focus.
- Auto-follow is the default, so the instructor does not carry the manual-switching burden.

None of this has been evaluated on an instructor station. It applies operator-display evidence to a new user.

Switching context safely also means guarding the instructor's actions and the boundaries between roles. For injection, the nuclear model points to a confirm step that shows the target, severity, ramp and trigger. An automatic snapshot taken just before each inject plays the part of the "return to interrupted scenario" slot. Batch scheduling lets several faults fire together, because the F-14 console could not do that by hand. For role boundaries, the F-14 battle-problem controller with no console position and Kongsberg's per-station visibility control both show that instructor, external-actor and assessor roles need their own views and permissions. The main truth leak to prevent is instructor-only content reaching students. In C2O that means the scenario generator, the ADCS failure viewer and the SIM voice loop are already intended to be professor-only. Truth views and instructor loops should be enforced on the server by role, not merely hidden in the client, and the instructor console should be visually unmistakable (distinct chrome, a persistent "INSTRUCTOR – TRUTH" band) so it is never projected to the class by accident. Freebies need a defined path too. A visible "sim anomaly" marker on the timeline lets the instructor declare a glitch as ignore, restart from snapshot, or work as real, following the Ames practice. Card-sorting the console's menu with experienced instructors and operators, then testing it with timed tasks as Branaghan did, is the cheapest way to check the navigation structure before building it out.

On implementation, a web stack is not the constraint. Measured benchmarks show SVG and Canvas performing about equally, degrading only above **about 400 nodes (about 8,000 elements)** ([Horak et al., IEEE VIS 2018](https://mt.inf.tu-dresden.de/cnt/uploads/Horak-2018-Graph-Performance-Poster.pdf)). An instructor console's mimics, causal graphs and state machines are far below that size, so SVG fits them, and DOM events and CSS theming come with it. Long rolling traces suit canvas. Updates should be batched per animation frame rather than drawn per message. Open MCT's Timeline, Timelist, EventTimestrip, PlanLayout and time Conductor are Apache-2 components that already implement a shared time axis ([Open MCT plugins.js](https://github.com/nasa/openmct/blob/master/src/plugins/plugins.js)). Whether they fit C2O's bilingual English–French requirement has not been checked. Accessibility of live SVG dashboards (colour-blind-safe alarm palettes, ARIA live regions) was not researched and needs its own pass.

## Conclusion

The literature supports more than a feature list for C2O: a clear position on what a spacecraft-ops instructor console is for. Space practice has treated the simulation officer as a skilled person working an engineering console. Other industries have shown that once the console must serve less specialised instructors, or run without one, the knowledge has to move into structure. That means curated inject catalogues, a separation of truth from indication, timed and conditional triggers, and scripted debriefs. C2O's own direction makes that shift unavoidable: web access, classes led by teaching assistants, and a solo practice mode in which software fills absent roles and chooses the faults. The same timeline, divergence model and trainee-load signals that help a human instructor are what an automated "director" would need. Building them as data, not as screens, serves both.

The weakest link is evidence, not ideas. No study found here measures instructor workload or situation awareness at a simulator console, or tests divergence highlighting, phase-driven pages or missed-cue alerts. C2O is in an unusual position to supply that evidence. Its classes produce repeated sessions with logged telecommands, alarms and injects, so it can compare event-to-acknowledgement latencies and instructor interventions across console designs. A modest built-in measurement layer would turn the console into a research instrument as well as a teaching tool.
